*The background in this section is adapted from Wikipedia's "Emergency fund" article (CC BY-SA 4.0), paraphrased rather than quoted.*

"Three to six months of expenses" is the number most commonly repeated as an emergency-fund
target, but per that background it was never a single universal formula — the right size depends on
how stable your income is, how many people depend on it, and how quickly you could replace it if
it stopped. A single earner with variable income and dependents typically needs more months covered
than a dual-income household with stable salaries; the commonly cited range is a starting point,
not a law.

What matters more than the label "three months" is what it concretely buys: at your recorded pace,
covering your target of **{{ef_target.formatted}}** would mean your household could absorb a full
stop in income for that long without touching debt or selling anything — including absorbing
several repeats of your largest single outflow, {{largest_outflow.formatted}}, without it becoming
a crisis.

You're currently at **{{ef_months_covered.formatted}}** months covered, against a runway (using all
liquid cash, not just the fund) of {{runway_days.formatted}} days. Those two numbers describe
related but different things: the emergency fund is what you've deliberately set aside for a shock;
runway is everything liquid, deliberate or not.

:::worked
A freelancer with irregular monthly income and a salaried employee with stable income both have
three months covered. The freelancer's three months protects against a much more likely event —
a slow month is common in freelance work — while the salaried employee's three months is protecting
against a rarer event, a sudden layoff. Same number, different amount of real protection, because
the underlying risk isn't the same.
:::

:::check
**Question:** Why isn't "three to six months" a one-size-fits-all rule?

- B. The right size depends on income stability, dependents, and how quickly income could be replaced
- C. It only applies to people who are self-employed
**Correct answer:** B — the commonly cited range is a starting heuristic, not a formula that fits every income and household situation equally.
