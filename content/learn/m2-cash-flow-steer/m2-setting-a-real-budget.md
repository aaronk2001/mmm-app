A budget that isn't compared against anything isn't a budget — it's a wish. The core mechanic that
makes budgeting work is variance: a stated target for a category, checked against what actually
happened, on a schedule you actually look at. Without the target, "actual" spending has nothing to
be judged against, so nothing ever counts as over or under.

Your current top variance categories — the ones furthest from whatever target (implicit or
explicit) they've been drifting against — are:

You have **{{monthly_surplus.formatted}}** in monthly surplus. A realistic budget doesn't try to
eliminate every variance at once — it picks the highest-leverage one or two categories, sets an
explicit number, and checks back next month. That's the entire loop: set a target, compare against
it, adjust the target or the behavior, repeat.

:::worked
A household sees a $180 variance in "Dining Out." Rather than setting an unrealistic $0 target,
they set a $250/month cap — enough to keep most of the behavior they value, tight enough to force a
real decision on the marginal meal out. Next month's actual spend becomes the new data point to
judge the target against.
:::

:::check
**Question:** Why does a budget need an explicit target, not just a record of spending?
- A. It doesn't — a spending record is sufficient on its own
- B. Without a stated target, there's nothing for "actual" spending to be compared against, so nothing counts as over or under
- C. Targets are only useful for fixed costs
**Correct answer:** B — variance, the thing that makes a budget actionable, requires both a target and an actual to compare.
:::
